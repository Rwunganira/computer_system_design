01

Two systems, one dataset

A textbook about system design is easy to read and hard to use, because its examples are about systems you have never built. This course anchors every chapter of Saltzer and Kaashoek in two small health systems that you will build, break and repair, module by module.

SurveilLite

A disease surveillance dashboard

Forty clinics in eight districts report weekly counts of measles, cholera and malaria. The dashboard turns them into incidence per 100,000 people by district and flags outbreaks.

VaxRecords

A vaccination records API

Health workers record each dose they give. The API keeps each child's vaccination history and each clinic's stock of vaccine.

Both run on one data module, course/data.py. It is short, and you will call it in almost every module:

The eight districts sit in two regions, North and South. Both regions have a district called Central. That is deliberate: names are not unique, and Module 3 is about exactly that problem. Each district therefore has an identifier, such as S-COA for Coastal in the South.

02

Same seed, same data

All the data is synthetic: no real patients, clinics or places. It is generated by a pseudo-random number generator, which is not random at all. Given the same starting value, the seed, it produces the same sequence of numbers every time.

That is what makes the course work. When your incidence table disagrees with the test, the difference is a real finding about your code, not noise from a different draw. Predict the last line before you run this.


      

      
Every generator in course/data.py takes a seed argument with a fixed default: case_reports(seed=42), population(seed=7), vaccinations(seed=11). Call one twice and you get identical tables. Pass a different seed and you get a different, equally plausible season.

      
Notice: the code on this page runs in your browser in Skulpt, whose random generator is not CPython's. The seed still fixes the sequence, but the numbers differ from the ones the repository's lab prints. The hero chart above uses the lab's exact numbers.

      

    


    
    

      
03
Building the weekly reports

      
Each clinic serves one fifth of its district's population, its catchment. Each disease has a baseline weekly rate per 100,000 people: measles 0.8, cholera 1.5, malaria 40. The generator turns a rate into an expected count for one clinic in one week, then draws an actual count from a Poisson distribution around it.

      
One district gets an outbreak. From week index 6 (the seventh week), cholera's expected count in Coastal is multiplied by 8, then 16, then 24, and so on. Choose a district, disease and week below and watch the expected count build up.

04
Incidence per 100,000

      
Raw counts mislead: Central South has 205,000 people and Highlands 67,000, so Central South should report about three times as many cases in a quiet week. SurveilLite divides by population and reports incidence per 100,000:

      
incidence = cases ÷ population × 100,000

      
The formula is the easy part. The design question is what to do with a district that reported no cases. The obvious loop only creates an entry when it sees a case. Predict how many districts end up in the table.

      

        

        

      

      
Six districts are simply absent. On a dashboard, a missing row reads as "no data": did Hillview report zero cholera, or did its report never arrive? An epidemiologist cannot tell. Listing every district with an explicit 0 makes the absence of cases a fact the system states, not a gap the reader has to guess about.

05
Finding the outbreak

      
Which district has a problem? The tempting answer is the one with the most cases. Run this on the lab's real weekly totals and predict which district has the most malaria.

      

        

        

      

      
Central South has the most malaria every season, because it has the most people. Nothing unusual is happening there. An outbreak is a change over time, not a large total. So compare each district with itself: split the reporting period into a first and second half, and look for the district whose cases grew the most.

Why add 1 to each half? A district with zero cases in the first half would divide by zero, and a district going from 0 to 1 case would get an infinite ratio. Adding 1 to both sides keeps small counts from dominating. It is a common epidemiological smoothing trick, and it is what the lab's solution does.


      

        
You tryBased on outbreak_district() in the lab.

        
Finish outbreak_district(reports, disease): sum each district's cases in the first and second half of the weeks, and return the district id with the largest (second + 1) / (first + 1). The generator from section 3 is included so you can test it; the checker also moves the outbreak to other districts and diseases.

        

          

        

      

      

    


    
    

      
06
What "working" means

      
Before you design a system, decide what it must do and what failure looks like. The two running systems fail in different ways, and the rest of the book is organised around those failures.

      

        
SurveilLite
A wrong number is worse than a slow page
An epidemiologist may send a response team on the strength of an incidence figure. A page that loads in eight seconds is annoying; a page that silently double-counts a district is dangerous.

        
VaxRecords
A lost record is a missed or repeated dose
If a dose record disappears, a child may be vaccinated twice, or not at all. The chapters on fault tolerance, atomicity and consistency are about exactly these failures.

pip install -e .
