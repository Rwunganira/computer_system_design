Computer System Design · Module 10

Two copies will disagree. The sync rule decides whether a dose survives.

The moment VaxRecords keeps a copy on a clinic tablet, readers can see old data and writers can collide. This module is about choosing what readers may see, and merging offline edits so that nothing a nurse entered quietly disappears.

01

One copy, then many

When there is one copy of the data, every read sees the last write. Once you make copies, that stops being automatic. VaxRecords already has several: a read replica of its database behind the SurveilLite dashboard, a cache of district totals, and a full copy on every clinic tablet that works for days without signal. You must decide what a reader of a copy is allowed to see. The book contrasts two answers.

Strict consistency

As if there were one copy

Every read returns the result of the most recent write. Easy to reason about, but a write is not finished until the replicas agree, and if one cannot be reached you must wait or refuse.

Eventual consistency

Agree in the end

Replicas may disagree for a while, but once updates stop they converge to the same value. Reads are fast and work offline; a reader can see stale data, and you need a rule for combining updates made in different places.

A nurse records MCV1 for a child at time 0, which raises the child's dose count from 5 to 6 on the primary. The dashboard reads from the replica once a second after that. Move the replication lag and switch between the two promises. Then cut the link to the replica.

Worked example

Neither promise is free. Eventual consistency answers at once and is sometimes wrong for a few seconds. 02
What does a stale answer cost?

      
Neither model is "correct". The deciding question is what a stale answer costs the person reading it. A SurveilLite incidence map that is an hour old leads to the same public health decision as a fresh one: weekly counts from 40 clinics do not change the picture in an hour. A nurse checking whether a child already had MCV1 before giving it cannot safely act on stale data, or must at least be told the data may be stale.

      
Ask of every figure: if this is a little out of date, who acts on it, and what goes wrong?

03
Caches and coherence

      
A cache is a replica whose job is speed. It is coherent if reads through it behave as if there were no cache. The book's tools are familiar from web work: invalidate the cached copy on every write, or bound staleness with a time-to-live (TTL) and accept that window. If SurveilLite caches district totals for five minutes, that is an explicit, bounded eventual-consistency promise.

04
Disconnected operation and last-writer-wins

      
VaxRecords runs on tablets in clinics that are offline for days. Each tablet keeps a full copy of the records it needs, health workers keep recording doses, and the tablet syncs when it reconnects. Now two replicas can change the same record independently. The book calls this the reconciliation problem, and it has two parts: detect that updates conflict, then resolve them.

      
Last-writer-wins (LWW) skips detection. Each record carries a timestamp, and the later one replaces the earlier one wholesale. It is easy, and it loses data. This is the scenario from the hero, written as the repository's offline_sync.py writes it. Tablet A records MCV1 at 10:00; tablet B records MCV2 at 10:15, but B's clock runs 30 minutes slow. 05
Version vectors detect conflicts

      
Version vectors detect conflicts without trusting clocks. Each replica counts its own updates. The vector {"server": 1, "tablet-A": 2} means "I have seen the server's first update and tablet A's first two". Missing entries count as 0. Comparing two vectors slot by slot gives one of four answers:

      

        
ComparisonMeaningAction

        
          
equalsame historynothing to do

          
before / afterone has seen everything the other hastake the newer one

          
concurrenteach has an update the other lacksreconcile

        
      

      
Change the counts and watch the verdict. A slot shown in the accent color is ahead of the same slot on the other side.

06
Resolving: the grow-only merge

      
Detection tells you that two updates conflict. Resolving them needs knowledge of the domain, not of the system. For doses the rule is simple: a dose, once given, stays given. Doses form a grow-only set, so the merge is a union, and the merged vector is the slot-by-slot maximum. Because union is commutative and idempotent, replicas can sync in any order, any number of times, and still converge.

      
Test that claim. Sync the two tablets in any order you like, as often as you like, and compare with last-writer-wins.

Not every field is that easy. If one tablet corrects a child's birth date to 2022-10-26 and another to 2022-10-16, union makes no sense and neither does "later wins": a person who knows the child has to decide. That is why good designs shape their data so that most updates are additions, like doses, and keep the few true conflicts visible for a human.

      
Replicas also exist for durability: several copies so a lost disk does not lose doses. The same question returns in a new form: after a write, how many replicas must have it before the tablet tells the health worker "saved"?
