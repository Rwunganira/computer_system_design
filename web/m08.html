Computer System Design · Module 8

Every part will fail. Make the system notice, then mask it.

01

Faults, errors and failures

Everyday speech uses "error" and "failure" for the same thing. The book separates three stages, and the separation is what makes the subject teachable:

StageWhat it isIn SurveilLite
Faultthe underlying defectone bit flips in the stored count for the week of 23 March
Errora wrong value inside the system, once the fault is activatedthe job that reads the file holds a Coastal total of 261 instead of 361
Failurethe system breaks its specification, as seen from outsidethe dashboard shows a cholera incidence that hides the outbreak

You know this shape already: exposure, infection, disease. Not every exposure infects, and not every infection causes disease. In the same way, a fault may never be activated (nobody reads the bad byte), and an error may be caught before anyone outside sees it. Fault tolerance is the work of breaking that chain, and the most valuable break is to detect an error before it spreads.

The dangerous fault is the latent one: the bad byte sitting unread on disk. Nothing tells you it is there. It waits until the dashboard reads it, and then it looks exactly like real data.

You trySort each event by the stage it belongs to.
Fault tolerance does not prevent faults. It stops a fault from becoming a failure, by detecting the error and then masking it.
02

Fail fast

A component that notices something impossible should stop and say so, rather than carry on and produce a plausible answer. This is fail-fast design. A negative case count or a district with a population of zero is not something to round, clip or skip quietly. It means some clinic report or some table is broken, and any incidence computed from it cannot be trusted.

The loud version produces no number at all, and that is the useful outcome: the message names report 502, so whoever reads the log can phone that clinic today. Failing fast trades a wrong answer for no answer plus a precise pointer to the fault.

03
Detect: a checksum next to the data

      
All fault tolerance comes down to redundancy: storing or computing more than the bare minimum. It is used in one of two ways. The first is error detection: keep a little extra information that lets you notice damage.

      
A checksum is a short value computed from the data. Save it next to the file; when you read the file back, compute it again and compare. If even one bit changed, the two values almost certainly differ. The local lab uses SHA-256 from Python's hashlib, which costs 64 bytes per file. Skulpt has no hashlib, so this page uses a small polynomial hash, h = (h * 31 + ord(ch)) % 1000000007, as a stand-in. It is far weaker against deliberate tampering, but it catches a flipped bit just the same.


      

        
Worked exampleThe last row of the file, before and after the bit flip.

        
StepCharh, originalCharh, flipped

        

      

      
The flipped bit changes one character, and from that character on every value of h is different. Because each step multiplies the running value by 31, a change anywhere in the file is carried all the way to the end.

      
Now the whole story in code. Bit 0 of the character '1' (byte 0011 0001) flips, and it becomes '0' (0011 0000). The file still parses, so int() raises nothing. Predict the total that a trusting reader computes.

      

        

        

      

      
A total of 261 instead of 361 is not off by a little. Bit flips land wherever they land, and the leading digit is as likely a target as any other. Now read the same damaged file the careful way.

      

        

      

      
Detection alone does not give you the right answer. It stops you publishing the wrong one, and that is already most of the value.

      

    


    
    

      
04
Mask: replicas and repair on read

      
The second use of redundancy is error masking: keep enough copies that you can still produce the right answer. With two checksummed replicas, a reader that finds one bad copy reads the other. That is what step 3 of the machine at the top of the page does.

      
Masking has a trap. If the reader quietly falls back to the backup and does nothing else, the bad primary stays bad. The next fault, on the backup this time, finds no good copy left. Faults accumulate silently until every copy is bad. So a good reader also repairs on read: it rewrites every bad replica from the good one, and logs that it did, because a masked fault is a safety margin you have just used up.

      

        
Mask only
Read the backup
Right answer today. The primary stays damaged; one more fault on the backup and the data is gone.

        
Mask and repair
Read the backup, rewrite the primary
Right answer today, and two good copies again. The time to repair shrinks from "whenever someone notices" to one read.

05
Masking computation: replicas and a voter

      
Checksums protect stored data. To protect a computation, run it more than once and compare. In N-modular redundancy, N replicas compute the same answer and a voter takes the majority. With three replicas this is triple modular redundancy (TMR).

      
In the lab, three replicas compute cholera incidence per 100,000 for each district. Replica B has a fault: its copy of the data stopped syncing in week 6, before the Coastal outbreak. Tap a replica to switch its data between current and stale, and watch the voter.

When is voting worse than one replica?

      
Suppose each replica is right with probability r, independently, and the voter itself never fails. TMR is right when at least two of the three are right: all three right, or exactly two right (three ways to choose the one that is wrong). If r is high, TMR is much better than one replica. If r is below a break-even point, voting makes things worse, because the majority is more often wrong than right.

06
Availability: MTTF and MTTR

      
Real components fail and get repaired, again and again. Two averages describe that cycle: MTTF, the mean time to failure, and MTTR, the mean time to repair. The fraction of time a component is up is its availability:

      
availability = MTTF / (MTTF + MTTR)

      
A system built from several parts combines their availabilities in one of two ways.

      

        
Series
All parts are needed
The dashboard needs power and the network and the app server and the database. Multiply: a1 * a2 * a3 * a4. The whole is less available than its weakest part.

        
Parallel
Any one part is enough
Two network links, either will do. The pair is down only when both are down: 1 - (1 - a1) * (1 - a2).

      

      
The lab's server room has four parts with invented but plausible MTTF and MTTR, in hours. The worst of them is up about 98% of the time. Predict the whole dashboard's availability, to three places.

      

        

        

      

      
About 230 hours a year, or nearly ten days. Every part in series adds its downtime. Now spend money on redundancy and repair, and see where it pays. Tick ×2 to put a second copy of a part in parallel; change MTTR to model a spare on the shelf or a better runbook.

Duplicating the two cheapest, least reliable parts takes the dashboard from about ten days down a year to about a day and a half (38 hours). Then the weakest remaining part dominates, and the other lever appears: halving the power supply's MTTR from 1 hour to 0.5 cuts another 9 hours. Shortening repair (a spare on the shelf, a runbook, an alert that wakes someone) improves availability as surely as lengthening MTTF, and is often far cheaper.

      
All of these formulas assume independent failures. One shared power supply under both app servers, one shared bug, or one administrator who makes the same mistake on both machines is a common-mode fault that parallel redundancy does not cover.

07
Design for the faults that will happen

      
The book's advice reduces to a handful of habits. Here they are, applied to SurveilLite and VaxRecords.

      

        
HabitIn practice

        
          
Check, do not trustAssume every disk, link and clinic report will eventually be wrong. Verify checksums; validate inputs.

          
Fail fastRefuse impossible values loudly, naming the report, instead of publishing a plausible wrong incidence.

          
Make masked faults visibleLog every outvoted replica and every repaired file. A masked fault is a lost safety margin.

          
Keep MTTR shortRepair automatically where you can (repair on read); alert a person where you cannot.

          
Hunt for common modesSame code, same data source, same power, same person. Each one turns "three copies" back into one.
