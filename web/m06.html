01

Measure first, then find the bottleneck

Four words carry the whole chapter. Latency is how long one request takes, start to finish: the epidemiologist clicking refresh feels latency. Throughput is how many requests finish per unit of time: the district office uploading 40 clinic reports at once cares about throughput. Overhead is work that is not the job itself, such as a network round trip or a disk flush; per item it often dwarfs the item. The bottleneck is the one stage that limits the whole.

The book's method is iterative: measure, find the bottleneck, apply one technique, measure again, and stop when the system is fast enough. The SurveilLite refresh in the lab does three things naively. It fetches 40 clinic reports one after another, saves 1,440 report rows to SQLite with a commit after each row, and computes district incidence from scratch 120 times. Before you look at a profile, commit to a guess.

Worked exampleThe lab's cProfile run, on one laptop.

Which stage do you think takes the most time?

Speeding up a stage saves at most the time that stage takes. That is why speeding up anything other than the bottleneck barely moves the total, and why fixing the bottleneck simply moves it somewhere else. Check the arithmetic with the measured numbers.

02
Batching: pay the overhead once

      
A SQLite commit waits until the disk confirms the data is safe. That wait is a fixed cost per commit, about 1.3 ms on the lab laptop, while the insert itself costs a few microseconds. Committing after every row pays the fixed cost 1,440 times. Batching pays it once for many items: in the lab, one transaction with executemany() saves the same 1,440 rows in 5 ms instead of 2.25 s.

03
Concurrency: overlap the waits

      
A fetch from a rural clinic spends nearly all of its 20 ms waiting on the network while the processor sits idle. Concurrency starts several fetches at once so that their waits overlap. It does not make any single fetch faster: latency per fetch is unchanged. It makes the whole set finish sooner: throughput goes up. In the lab, 10 threads fetch the 40 reports in 0.12 s instead of 0.92 s.

04
Caching, and the price of a stale copy

      
A cache keeps a copy of a result close by so you do not compute or fetch it again. It is judged by its hit rate, and it works when requests have locality: a small working set used again and again. The dashboard shows 40 clinics × 3 diseases = 120 incidence figures, but each clinic shows its district's figure, and there are only 8 districts. So the working set is 8 × 3 = 24 values, used 120 times.

      
The price is that a copy can go stale. A cache needs an invalidation rule, or new outbreak reports never reach the page. Build the page, then send a new cholera report from Coastal and build it again, with and without invalidation.

05
Dallying and speculation

      
Two more techniques in the book's list play with when work is done, rather than how.

      

        
Dallying
Delay, hoping to batch or skip
Write dose records to a buffer and flush every few seconds. Many records share one disk wait, and a record corrected within the window is written once. The cost: a record waits up to one interval, and a crash loses what is still in the buffer.

        
Speculation
Do it early, betting it will be needed
Precompute this week's incidence table as soon as the last clinic reports, before anyone opens the dashboard. The cost: wasted work if nobody looks, or if a late correction arrives.

      

      
Dallying turns a stream of single writes into batches. Dose records arrive at random, about two a second, for one minute, and the buffer is flushed every 5 seconds if it holds anything.

      

        

        

      

      
Over a hundred records cost a dozen disk waits, and no record waits more than 5 seconds. Whether 5 seconds is acceptable is a question for the application: fine for a dose register, too slow for an alarm.

      

    


    
    

      
06
Queues and Little's law

      
Every service with a waiting line is a queue: requests at a web server, children at a vaccination clinic. Little's law relates three averages in any stable queue:

      
N = X × R. The average number inside equals the throughput times the average time each one spends inside.

      
It needs no assumption about how arrivals or service times are distributed, so it is a quick sanity check on any capacity plan. Knowing any two gives the third. The one trap is units: arrivals are usually per hour and times in minutes.

07
Why you plan for utilisation well below 1

      
Little's law tells you the average. It does not tell you how long the line gets. This simulation is the lab's clinic_queue.py: children arrive at random, 90 an hour, each dose takes 2.5 minutes on average, first come first served, for an 8-hour day. It counts the children inside by watching the door (+1 at each arrival, −1 at each departure) and compares that with X × R.

      

        

      

      
Two lessons. First, the counted N matches X × R in every row: Little's law holds whatever the staffing. Over a window that starts and ends empty, the two are the same area counted two ways, which is why the law needs no assumptions. Your browser's random numbers differ from the lab's (the lab prints R = 5.8 minutes for 4 vaccinators), but the shape is the same.

      
Second, look at R. At 47% utilisation a child spends 2.5 minutes inside, the dose and nothing else. At 94%, more than double, and the peak line is long. At 125% (3 vaccinators) the line grows all day and children wait about an hour. Plot it:

      

        

      

      
This is pylab in a browser subset: plot, axhline, title and friends behave as in matplotlib on these calls, and draw into the output. The curve is flat, then bends sharply upward as utilisation nears 1. Plan for utilisation well below 1, and size the waiting area from N plus room for the peak, not from the arrival rate.
